## 0. Bottom line

1. **The general slogan is old; the depth instance is not.** "Obfuscated for humans but recognisable by machines" was established for faces two decades ago (Newton, Sweeney & Malin, TKDE 2005 [V]; McPherson, Shokri & Shmatikov 2016 [V]; Oh et al. ECCV 2016 [V-cite]) and for whole sensor classes by MultiGait (Sep 2026 [V]) and by Strufe's group's "A False Sense of Privacy" (PoPETs 2024 [V]). The manuscript must present its thesis as a **depth-specific, mechanistic, evaluation-methodology** instance, citing these as the lineage, not as a new principle.
2. **Nobody has done the depth-specific de-identification test.** Two arXiv sweeps plus OpenAlex found no paper that evaluates identity leakage of depth precision reduction, depth person-masking, or depth silhouettes as privacy controls. The nearest are RGB (Wu et al. ECCV 2018), VR telemetry (Nair et al. 2024) and gait-silhouette anonymisers (Tieu 2019 [V]; Hirose TIFS 2022 [V]; GaitProtector 2026 [V]). C2's "precision reduction and person-masking are not privacy controls" therefore survives as new **for depth**, with "to our knowledge".
3. **Mucha & Kampel (ICCHP-AAATE 2022; PETRA 2022) are the only prior depth-privacy critiques and they conclude the other way.** They tested face recognition only, at near range, and concluded depth is "more private than RGB", with disclosure possible only for "less than 100" individuals and high-precision sensors [V]. The manuscript's body-scale, cross-session, 2.5-s, per-person results directly test that residual condition. This is the main depth-specific positioning hook.
4. **Legal framing: the sensor does not decide the legal category; the processing does.** GDPR Art. 4(14) as quoted by EDPB 3/2019 §74, Recital 51, EDPB §76 three criteria [V]; the EU AI Act Recital 15 explicitly lists "body shape … gait, posture" among features of biometric identification [V]; the EDPB-EDPS called for a ban on recognition of "faces but also of gait" in public spaces [V]. In the US, BIPA's closed list excludes "physical descriptions such as height, weight" [V] and Zellmer v. Meta (9th Cir. 2024) made **capability to identify** the test [V]. None of the regulator texts examined (AI Act, EDPB 3/2019, CNIL 2022, WP193, JO 5/2021, EP 2021, HHS) mentions depth cameras, 3D cameras, thermal cameras or LiDAR (0 hits) [V]: the "depth is non-identifying" premise lives in engineering practice and HIPAA Safe-Harbor reading, not in any guidance.
5. **The premise-in-the-wild catalogue has a rhetorical asset:** the same institutions that state depth prevents identification also published depth identification of the same populations (Stanford: Haque CVPR 2016 vs Haque MLHC 2017 / Luo MLHC 2018; Missouri: Banerjee EMBC 2012 "Resident identification using Kinect depth image data" vs Banerjee EMBC 2014 / Rantz 2014). Present this neutrally as evidence that practitioners treat identification as a feature when they want it and as impossible when they do not.

## C. Legal / normative statements (with what they imply for the manuscript)

C1. **GDPR Art. 4(14) / Art. 9 / Recital 51.** EDPB Guidelines 3/2019 §74 quotes Art. 4(14): biometric data is data "resulting from specific technical processing relating to the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics" that "allow or confirm the unique identification"; and states video footage "cannot however in itself be considered as biometric data" absent such processing [V edpb2019.txt]. §76 gives three criteria (nature, means "specific technical processing", purpose "uniquely identifying") [V]. §80–82: detecting characteristics to classify does not fall under Art. 9; storing templates to recognise a returning person does [V]. Recital 51: photographs are biometric data "only when processed through a specific technical means allowing the unique identification" [V gdpr-info.eu]. **Implication:** raw depth video of a person is personal data regardless of sensor; it becomes Art. 9 biometric data the moment a controller processes it to identify — which is exactly what the manuscript demonstrates is feasible with 2.5 s of depth. The premise conflates "not a photograph" with "not identifiable".

C2. **EU AI Act (Reg. 2024/1689).** Art. 3(34) defines biometric data as in C1; Art. 3(35) "biometric identification" = "automated recognition of physical, physiological, behavioural, or psychological human features" for establishing identity [V aiact.txt]. **Recital 15** lists the features: "the face, eye movement, body shape, voice, prosody, gait, posture, heart rate…" [V]. Recital 14 ties the notion to GDPR Art. 4(14) [V]. Art. 5(1)(g) prohibits biometric categorisation inferring protected attributes [V]; Annex III point 1(a) makes remote biometric identification systems high-risk [V]. Prohibitions applied from 2 Feb 2025; the remainder from 2 Aug 2026 [V timeline extract]. **Implication:** body-shape/gait recognition from depth is, by name, biometric identification in EU law; the manuscript can say this without interpretation.

C3. **EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 5/2021, §32:** calls for "a general ban on any use of AI for an automated recognition of human features in publicly accessible spaces - such as of faces but also of gait" [V jo2021.txt]. Gait is treated by both EU supervisors as a biometric to be regulated like the face.

C4. **European Parliament study (JURI/PETI, 2021), "Biometric Recognition and Behavioural Detection":** "Weak biometrics are features that are 'less unique' or 'less stable', e.g. body shape, behavioural patterns, voice" versus strong identifiers (fingerprint, iris) and soft biometrics (gender, age) [V ep2021.txt]. **Implication:** the manuscript's per-subject identifiability (0–100 %), cohort curve (K = 4→28) and cross-session decay are the empirical content of "less unique / less stable" — it can position itself as quantifying the weak-biometric category for depth.

C5. **Article 29 WP Opinion 3/2012 (WP193):** behavioural biometrics include "gait analysis, way of walking or moving"; soft biometrics are "very common traits not suitable to clearly distinguish or identify" but "enhancing the performance of other identification systems" [V wp193.txt]. Height/width-type anthropometry sits on this boundary; the manuscript's 13-scalar floor (89 % under frame-random, collapsing across sessions) is a direct measurement of where.

C6. **ICO, Biometric data guidance (Biometric recognition):** "Biometric recognition describes when you use biometric data to uniquely identify someone"; "If you are using a biometric recognition system, you are processing special category biometric data"; aligned to ISO/IEC 2382-37:2022 [V ico_page.html]. Same processing-based logic as C1.

C7. **CNIL, "Caméras dites 'intelligentes' ou 'augmentées'" (July 2022):** augmented cameras are distinguished from biometric processing (Art. 9), but even where images are anonymised or destroyed almost immediately the operations "constituent un traitement de données à caractère personnel si les images contiennent des personnes"; anonymisation must prevent re-identification by "moyens raisonnables" [V cnil2022.txt]. **Implication:** "we only keep depth" is not anonymisation; the reasonable-means test is an attack evaluation — which is what the manuscript supplies.

C8. **United States.** BIPA, 740 ILCS 14/10: "Biometric identifier" means "a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry" and excludes "physical descriptions such as height, weight, hair color, or eye color" and information "captured from a patient in a health care setting" [V ilga.html]. Zellmer v. Meta Platforms (9th Cir. 2024): "face signatures cannot identify and therefore are not biometric identifiers" — capability to identify is the statutory test [V zellmer.txt]. HIPAA Safe Harbor (Q)/(P)/(R) as in A. **Implication:** depth body-shape/gait identification is outside BIPA's closed list (and health-care captures are carved out), yet the Zellmer capability test is precisely what the manuscript measures; in a US hospital the only hook is Safe Harbor (R) "any other unique identifying … characteristic". State this as a gap between statute and capability, not as legal advice.

**Legal synthesis for the paper (one paragraph):** the anonymity premise is a category error — the law attaches "biometric" to the processing and its identifying capability, not to whether a human can read the image; EU instruments now name body shape and gait explicitly; no regulator has ever said depth is non-identifying; US statutes leave depth-derived body biometrics largely unregulated while making capability the test. MultiGait already draws the "same legal framework" conclusion [V]; the manuscript should cite it and add the specific instruments (Recital 15, EDPB §74/76, CNIL reasonable-means, BIPA exclusion, Safe Harbor (Q)/(R)), which MultiGait does not do (its only GDPR mentions are the Art. 9 consent form and one sentence in the discussion [V]).

## E. What the thesis can still claim as new after MultiGait — and what it must not

**CAN claim (with the cited lineage):**
1. The first **mechanistic decomposition of why depth identification looks near-perfect**: protocol ladder R0→R4 with a trivial-cue floor (89 % under frame-random), block/guard sweeps at matched n_train, replicated on three corpora/sensors. MultiGait reports the 98 %→60–80 % drop but does not dissect it and calls cross-session generalisation an open gap [V]; no 2020+ paper quantifies frame-random vs session-disjoint inflation for vision person-ID (06). Lineage: Hanisch 2024 "False Sense" (evaluation methodology), Hammerla & Plötz 2015 (HAR adjacency), Georgiev 2022 / Melzi 2023 (touch/ECG).
2. The first **depth-specific test of two would-be privacy controls** — precision reduction (16→1 bit at fixed range, silhouette as the 1-bit limit) and person-masking (exact-complement inpainting, hole) — showing neither is a control. No depth de-identification or identity-leakage evaluation exists (two sweeps here + 06). Lineage: Nair 2024 (VR precision), Wu 2018 (RGB degradations), Xiao 2021 (hole leak, RGB), Tieu/Hirose/GaitProtector (silhouette anonymisers).
3. The **deployment-facing operating point**: 2.5-s observation budget, validity gate (74 % of walking frames clip the body; 89.5 mm/m drift vs 105 mm between-subject SD), top-k, per-person identifiability 0–100 %, cohort curve K = 4→28. None of these appear in MultiGait (0 hits for seconds/clip/per-person) [V]. This is the empirical content of the EP 2021 "less unique / less stable" category (C4) and the direct test of Mucha & Kampel's "less than 100 individuals" residual condition (B2). Cite Friedman et al., arXiv:1906.06272 (gallery-size dependence) [V extract] for the cohort curve.
4. **Metric anthropometry in millimetres as the mechanism** (unprojected depth + sensor mask; 19 % single-frame cross-session, beating every CNN), linked to the uniqueness literature (Godil 2004; Lucas & Henneberg 2016: body more variable than face) and to Banerjee 2012's shape descriptors — which nobody has connected to the depth-privacy debate.
5. **Pre-registered negative results** on fusion, normals, dynamic-range rescaling, multi-frame input fusion, gait on BIWI, and the room-confounded duplication correlation.
6. **The legal reading** in C (sensor ≠ category; Recital 15 names body shape/gait; BIPA exclusion; Safe Harbor inference) — Mucha 2022 flagged "an open legal problem" for faces only; MultiGait states the conclusion without the instruments.
7. **The premise catalogue with its internal contradictions** (A vs B5/B7): same sensor, same groups, opposite claims — presented neutrally.

**MUST NOT claim:**
1. That depth (or any "privacy-friendly" sensor) identifies people, or identifies them across sessions — MultiGait (199/8/3) [V], Haque 2016, Wu 2017, Karianakis 2018, Dubois 2015, Banerjee 2012 all precede.
2. That "illegible to humans ≠ anonymous to machines" is a new principle — Newton/Sweeney/Malin 2005, McPherson 2016, Oh 2016, Mucha 2022 ("from a human perspective"), MultiGait/Hanisch "false sense of privacy". Frame it as the depth instance and cite the lineage in the first paragraph.
3. That depth is "as identifying as RGB" or "not more private than RGB" — Mucha found RGB FR ≫ depth FR [V]; the manuscript's own C4 shows RGB +45 pp within session. The defensible claim is "depth is not anonymous", plus "RGB's edge is clothing and vanishes across sessions".
4. First depth face recognition, or first to raise depth privacy concerns in AAL/hospitals — Mucha & Kampel 2022, Chou 2018, Srivastav 2019.
5. Any human-observer result ("illegible to humans") unless measured — the manuscript has no perception study; Korshunov 2012, BDQ 2022, Mucha 2022 (acceptance 8/13) did. Use "illegible" as motivation with citations, not as a finding.
6. Largest cohort, most sensors, longest interval — MultiGait.
7. An identity attack on actual bathroom/care-home depth recordings — BIWI, TVRID and the in-house corpus are not such data (06 confirms none exists). Say "deployment-representative sensors and geometry (top-view D455, Kinect v1/Azure)".
8. A categorical legal status ("body shape is/is not biometric data") — GDPR depends on processing; AI Act names it; BIPA excludes it. Report the instruments, not a verdict.
9. Novelty of background ablations or of depth's clothing invariance (02 report).
